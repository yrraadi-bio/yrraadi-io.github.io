How do we know who will respond?

Data engine

By mapping tumor biology using real patient tissue.

Every tumor we profile adds a datapoint capturing intra- and inter-tumoral heterogeneity. Computational analysis tools, biomarkers and deep learning systems can be used to model and generalize this distribution of malignant tissue. Underlying it is high-signal data, at scale.

Improve outcomes in the clinic.

Optimized trials cost less and reach more patients.

Bringing one cancer drug to market costs a median of $2.8 billion. Most of the people who take the drugs below do not respond to them, and every percentage here is already measured inside the eligible group.

Predicting response before enrollment moves both numbers. A trial run on predicted responders is a smaller trial with a larger effect size, which is the largest single lever on what a drug costs to develop. And the patients it screens out stop being noise and become populations: distinct subgroups, each with the biology some other drug acts on, each one more people treated and a market that does not exist today.

None of these came with a way to tell in advance who would respond. Three list no marker at all; PD-L1 was reported as neither prognostic nor predictive in the trials that used it; RAS testing arrived after Erbitux was approved and narrowed the eligible group without finding the responders inside it. Where response was this rare, approval came on survival, or on how long the responses lasted, rather than on how often they came. Each rate is measured in its own trial's eligible group, so the rates are not comparable between rows, and revenue is worldwide 2025 as each manufacturer reported it.
